Bibhu Acharya · 2009

Proud to Be an Indian

Taking a puff at the ‘death stick’, I opened my newly bought diary, the one I loved because of the leather-look lining, even though I had many at home. I sipped through the cool mocha sitting in the cafe in Chongming Island in China. There has had been a clutter in my mind; the clutter that clinched my mind with anxiety. I wanted to drain it out into a bottle – the pale pages of the diary. But I needed a funnel. I asked the waitress and she happily lent her pen.

They say India is the largest democracy in the world. Democracy, as described by Abraham Lincoln, is of the people, by the people and for the people.

I served the nation for five years in the Indian Navy. I quit Navy five years back and came abroad. But deep down inside I can still feel a touch of patriotism even though I try my best to dilute myself in the ways of this country. When in Rome, do as the Romans do. But every time someone questions against the valour of my nationality, I have been standing up strongly against him since last five years – the five years which seem like ages. Among them are some questions that are really funny like for e.g. the way we eat with our hands instead of spoons, forks, knives and chopsticks, my inability to sing and dance like the glamorous movie stars in the movies, my not being dark-skinned like other Indians, the perennial heat in India etc. Some even confuse me with Indonesians instead of being from Indu, the Chinese name for India. With a smile of pity for them, I do clear their doubts. Sometimes I get agitated. But I cannot help their unawareness. The only thing I can contribute to them is clearing their doubts. But if the same doubts are displayed from my country men, that not only makes me agitated and pitiful but also torments me.

Couple of months back, I travelled to my homeland for vacation. As I was waiting for the announcement for boarding the flight, my mobile phone shouted at me, “Guess what sexy, you have mail”, (That was my ringtone). It sometimes queered me as to the numerous things we can do with this small equipment now days. I checked the message and was informed of the terrorist attack in Mumbai. I was travelling from Shanghai to New Delhi. The final destination of the flight was Mumbai – the dreaded destination at the moment. A wave of surprise and sorrow grasped me I hadn’t yet quite recovered from this shock when I heard a voice with a German accent “Is it safe in New Delhi now?” Across my chair seated was a German guy, who, I learned later, was going to New Delhi for his internship. I felt shamed at his question. With a defensive tone I replied, “Yes of course. It is safe in New Delhi”, quite unsure of my answer. He informed me that two German nationals were also among the hundred people killed in this agonising mishap. He said, “You, of course, feel free to go to your homeland; but for me, I am still confused.” Somehow I winded up the discussion concluding that he should not fear, and went back to my book that I was reading. As I went through the lines, I realised I was reading about freedom. The lines read:

...Freedom should come first from the depth of your mind and then onwards...

Quite perturbed by these thoughts, I stepped inside the flight. As I settled down in my seat near the window, I had a glance outside at the water droplets on the wings. The droplets that were quite unaware of their fate lay still as the flight prepared for its journey. There were two things that I was quite particular about when I check-in. One, window seat as I liked to view the vastness of the various landscapes, the cotton clouds, and the endless horizon where you can always see light during night, the slow vibration of the wings during turbulence and the fate of those droplets and the droplets that came along the journey, second, a double meal so as to keep up with my over-average diet standard.

The air in the cabin and along the aisle had started changing itself into the sweet and nostalgic fragrance. The sound of all the different languages relieved me for some time off the perturbation. I saw the chief airhost taking care of all the passengers onboard with a nice smile on his face. There was one man struggling with his tonnes of luggage to stuff them in the limited cabin-space above him. My poor memory though recalled him to be some actor from the movies when I saw him trying his best to draw some attention from his neighbours. There was an elderly couple who seemed unhappy with this man as they fell short of cabin- space. A young and beautiful lady, who seemed like some journalist by her quite large and obvious eye- glasses, was seated ahead of me. She turned around and asked me politely if I had some Indian currencies in exchange of a one-thousand rupees note. Sadly I apologised. Every one gradually settled down and we took off. I leaned my forehead against the window glass trying to relish the view of the fate of the water droplets.

When the seat belt sign was turned off, I got up and tried to steer through the passage way to the toilet as the path was blocked by many bags, thanks to the so-infamous actor onboard. The meals were served and as usual I received only one. Not surprised by their forgetful habits, I requested for the second one. I started eating. I could hear the chief airhost asking the passengers on his way if they were enjoying. I was quite impressed by his ability as he was speaking five different languages with different people. He must be quite smart, I thought. Someone asked him about the terrorist attacks and he consoled them through their fear. He advanced towards me, gave me my second meal and said, “Here is your meal Sir and my apologies for the inconvenience.” I thanked him with a smile of appreciation. Quite pleased he asked me where was I from. I told him I was from Orissa. He smiled and replied, “But, of course. I had guessed you would be from that part of the country, like Bangladesh.” Bangladesh? Did I hear him correct? Did he really say Bangladesh? With a bit of hesitation I said, “Beg your pardon!” He replied, “Yes, I thought you would be from that part of India like Bangladesh.”

Shocked, as I was, but trying not to question his intelligence, the intelligence of a man who could speak five different languages, I twitched my lips for an unwanted smile and concentrated on my meal. I was taken aback. Such unawareness reminded me of the same torment again.

I returned back to China. For a couple of months I was drowned in the busy schedule at office. I worked at a shipyard on the island. Generally we have lunch at the shipyard’s canteen. I enjoy the food even if the food habits are different. Sometimes I come across few Indians who, like me, were ship-owner’s representatives working as supervisors. We cross each other’s paths quite a few times in a day and I never forget to exchange a few words or at least a smile as we feel connected.

Once as I was discussing some trivial matters with my Chinese colleague after we finished our meal, one of them approached our table and placing his hands on my shoulder asked with a smile of content, “Indian?” I immediately reciprocated with a wide smile, “Yes!” Then we discussed our purpose of being in the shipyard, our modes of entertainment in the island, our address in the island and in the office etc. I learnt he was from the southern part of India and was supposedly highly qualified with immense experience. I tried, as I always do, to add humour to the conversation and invited him to visit our site office. He agreed with a delight. Suddenly he enquired about my origin in India and humorously i snapped back, “does that really matter?’ This was just an attempt to avoid the direction in which the conversation was going. He smiled and replied, “The first day I saw you, I was sure that you were Indian. And I think you are from Bangladesh right?” For a moment I felt as if time had stopped two steps ahead of me and gave me a wicked but smiling stare. Somewhere deep inside my inner-self a weird quake shook me all over. The revengeful argument that I had kept aside against the ‘intelligent air host’ from the flight was desperate to erupt on this man. But trying to bring myself to ‘self’ I answered coolly, “No, India.” His smile on the face eloped for a moment and then he pulled it back on his face and said, “Yes I know India, but from Bangladesh. Right?” I stood up, extended my hands for a good-bye shake and said, “Sorry, you have to excuse us as we have to leave now. See you around.” And we hurried out the canteen leaving him confused. I thought it was better that way.

And now as I was almost done with my coffee and wanted to wind up this experience, a cute little waitress comes up to me and says in Chinese, “Sir, your bill is 38 Yuan.” As I put my hands in my pocket to pull out the money, I noticed her desperation to collect some words and courage to express something. I stopped myself for a moment as I needed to concentrate. Neither of us spoke each other’s language. As I struggled to understand, I realised she was talking about the Indian women clad in sarees with a red dot on the forehead looked really beautiful. All Indians had big eyes and long eyelashes which made her envious. The funny way of tossing the dough in the air by the chefs in big restaurants while preparing the Indian bread, and the singing and dancing movie stars in the movies made her crazy about India. She was aware of the major big cities like Mumbai, Shin (New in Chinese) Delhi, Chennai and Kolkata. She also knew cities like Bangaluru and Goa. She asked me where I was from and I, trying to make it simpler for her, said that I am from nearby Kolakata. She gave me a big smile. Neither could she speak five different languages nor did she have immense experience. But she had the knowledge of very simple but mostly unnoticed by many of us, things. She even recited two lines from a very famous Hindi song from one of the Bollywood movies and even danced few steps quite enthusiastically. I was speechless. I guess that last drop had funnelled into the bottle. The bottle was full and so was my heart.
